Start with your life, not a product

Here's what could knock your plans off course — and what to do about it.

A good financial plan starts with what's actually happening in your life right now, then looks at where that tends to create risk, and ends with a short list of moves that keep things on track. Pick the stage below that sounds like you, using the buttons at the bottom of the screen, and we'll take it from there.

Life stage

Launching

You're at the early stages of your career, haven't formed a family yet, and may not own a home of your own.

Worth asking yourself

Most people your age either have zero coverage or way too little — let's see which one you are.

What we'll cover

Protection

If you carry student loans, a car loan, or credit card debt and something happened to you, who is left holding it — a co-signer, a parent, no one?

"I don't need life insurance, I have no kids" ignores that debt and co-signers don't disappear. Reframe protection around who inherits the liability, not just who inherits the loss of income.

What you can do about it

  • Confirm exactly which debts have a co-signer and whether they'd be legally responsible if you couldn't pay.
  • Price a small term life policy sized to your outstanding debt, not a full income-replacement amount.
  • Lock in a rate while you're young and healthy — it only gets more expensive later.
Protection

If you were diagnosed with a terminal illness, how would you pay for treatment and everyday expenses — especially if it also cost you your job?

Medical costs and lost income often hit at the same time. Health insurance may cover treatment, but few people plan for the paycheck gap layered on top of it.

What you can do about it

  • Check your health plan's out-of-pocket maximum so a worst case has a real number attached to it.
  • Look into critical illness or supplemental health coverage that pays a lump sum on diagnosis, on top of medical coverage.
  • Pair that with disability income coverage so lost wages are covered separately from the medical bills.
Protection

If you were diagnosed with a serious illness tomorrow and couldn't work for six months, could you still pay rent and keep your lifestyle — or would you need help?

Single earners have no second income to fall back on. This is the fastest way to make protection concrete and personal instead of abstract.

What you can do about it

  • Price out an individual disability income policy — it protects the paycheck itself, not just what happens if you die.
  • Ask your employer about short- and long-term disability coverage you may already have, and check the benefit amount.
  • Build toward 3-6 months of expenses in savings so a coverage gap doesn't immediately become a rent problem.
Liquidity

What growth rate is your emergency fund actually earning — is it keeping pace with inflation, or just sitting there losing value?

An emergency fund still has to be liquid first, but parking it in an account that pays next to nothing quietly loses purchasing power every year it sits there.

What you can do about it

  • Move emergency savings into a high-yield savings account instead of a standard checking or low-rate savings account.
  • Compare a few online banks' current savings rates — the difference is often more than 1% a year for no added risk.
  • Keep the funds liquid and FDIC-insured; growth rate matters less than being able to access the money instantly.
Liquidity

If your car broke down and you lost your job in the same month, do you have 3-6 months of expenses saved — or would one bad month land on a credit card?

At this stage liquidity is usually the first real gap: income is growing but savings haven't caught up yet, and there's no partner's income to absorb a shock.

What you can do about it

  • Open a dedicated high-yield savings account so emergency money isn't mixed with everyday spending.
  • Set an automatic transfer each payday, even a small one, so the fund builds without relying on willpower.
  • Target 3-6 months of essential expenses before aggressively investing extra cash.
Accumulation

Are you contributing enough to get the full employer 401(k) match, or is that free money currently left on the table?

This is the lowest-friction accumulation win available: no budget change required, just capturing money that's already been offered.

What you can do about it

  • Check your plan's match formula and confirm your contribution percentage actually captures the full match.
  • If you can't afford the full match yet, set a reminder to raise your contribution with each raise.
  • Confirm your investment elections inside the plan aren't sitting in cash by default.
Longevity/Legacy

If something happened to you, would anyone actually know your wishes — do you have a beneficiary named anywhere, or a will?

Most people this age assume they're too young for this to matter. The point isn't the document, it's that decisions get made by default (state law, whoever's listed on an old form) instead of on purpose.

What you can do about it

  • Name a beneficiary on every account that allows it — 401(k), IRA, life insurance, bank accounts.
  • Draft a simple will, even a basic one; it's far better than leaving the decision to state default rules.
  • Revisit both any time your relationship status changes.

Life stage

Building

You've paired your life with someone else's — sharing a home, a budget, or plans for a family — even if kids aren't part of the picture yet.

Worth asking yourself

Now that your finances are linked, does your coverage actually reflect what your household needs — or is it still what you had when you were single?

Start here

  • A joint protection review timed to a major event such as marriage or a home purchase.

What we'll cover

Protection

Have you talked about what happens financially if one of you passes away unexpectedly — not just emotionally, but literally: whose name is on what, and who inherits it?

Couples at this stage have usually discussed the emotional side of loss but rarely the mechanical side: accounts, titling, and who has access to what.

What you can do about it

  • Review how each account is titled and who's named as beneficiary — update anything left over from before the relationship.
  • Get term life insurance sized to replace the income the household actually depends on.
  • Write down where key documents and account logins are so the surviving partner isn't searching blind.
Protection

If one of you couldn't work due to illness or injury, could the household still cover rent or the mortgage on one income alone?

Two incomes often mean two lifestyles built on the assumption both keep coming in. Protection here is testing that assumption before life does.

What you can do about it

  • Add up what one income alone actually covers each month, and where the shortfall would be.
  • Look at disability coverage for both partners, not just the higher earner.
  • Size the emergency fund to the higher of your two monthly budgets, not the lower.
Liquidity

Do you have a shared emergency fund, or is each of you quietly assuming the other person has savings to fall back on?

"Both work, so we're covered" is a common but untested assumption. Ask each partner separately what they believe the number is — the gap is often the real finding.

What you can do about it

  • Ask each other, separately, what you think the emergency fund balance is — then compare answers.
  • Open one shared account for emergencies so the responsibility isn't assumed onto either partner alone.
  • Agree on what actually counts as an emergency before you need the money.
Accumulation

Are you both saving toward the same goals — a home, kids, retirement — or making decisions independently that could conflict later?

This stage is where separate financial habits either merge into a plan or quietly compound into two different plans that collide down the road.

What you can do about it

  • Sit down together and list your top three goals in priority order, not just individually.
  • Consolidate or coordinate savings so you're not unintentionally working against each other.
  • Revisit the list once a year, since priorities shift as the relationship does.
Longevity/Legacy

If you're planning a life together — or a family — have you updated beneficiary designations and a will to reflect that, or are they still set from before you met?

Old beneficiary forms are one of the most common and easiest-to-fix gaps: a five-minute update that prevents money going to the wrong person entirely.

What you can do about it

  • Pull up every account's current beneficiary and confirm it still matches who you'd want today.
  • Update your will (or create one) to reflect your current relationship and any new dependents.
  • Repeat this check after any major life event — marriage, a child, a move.

Life stage

Growing family

You're raising kids at home, juggling school, activities, and a career, with less time than ever to think about money.

Worth asking yourself

If something happened to you tomorrow, would your family's day-to-day life actually stay the same — same house, same school, same routine?

Start here

  • Naming a guardian and reviewing beneficiaries — free, fast, and often reveals the family has never actually finished this.

What we'll cover

Protection

Have you named a guardian for your kids in writing, or is that still just a conversation you've had?

Naming a guardian and reviewing beneficiaries is free, fast, and often reveals the family has never actually finished this — a quick win that opens the door to the bigger conversation.

What you can do about it

  • Name a guardian in your will, not just in conversation — an unwritten wish isn't legally binding.
  • Talk to that person directly and confirm they're willing and able.
  • Review beneficiary designations at the same time; it takes minutes.
Protection

If something happened to you tomorrow, would your family's day-to-day life actually stay the same — same house, same school, same routine — or would something have to give?

This is the question that makes protection real for parents: not abstract "coverage," but whether the kids' world stays stable.

What you can do about it

  • Add up the true cost of keeping your kids' routine unchanged — housing, school, activities — for at least a year.
  • Get term life insurance sized to that number, not just a round figure.
  • Review coverage again each time your mortgage, school, or family size changes.
Liquidity

If one parent had to stop working for a few months to care for a sick child, how long could the household sustain that on savings alone?

Families at this stage often have some savings, but rarely have sized it against a real caregiving scenario rather than a generic "emergency."

What you can do about it

  • Estimate how many months of expenses caregiving leave would require, using your employer's actual leave policy.
  • Build the emergency fund toward that specific number, not just a generic 3-6 months.
  • Check whether either employer offers paid family leave you haven't factored in.
Accumulation

Are you saving for your kids' education in a way that competes with your own retirement savings — or protects it?

Kids can borrow for school; parents can't borrow for retirement. Surfacing this trade-off early prevents an accidental choice made by default years later.

What you can do about it

  • Set up separate accounts for education and retirement so one doesn't quietly fund the other.
  • Automate retirement contributions first, then direct what's left to education savings.
  • Remember that loans exist for college and not for retirement when the two goals compete.
Longevity/Legacy

If both parents were gone, is there a plan for who manages the money left behind for your kids — not just who raises them?

Families plan for a guardian far more often than for a financial guardian. The two decisions are separate and both need an answer.

What you can do about it

  • Decide who would manage the money left behind — a trustee or executor — separately from who raises the kids.
  • Put that decision in writing through a will or trust.
  • Make sure the guardian and the money manager both know the plan, even if they're the same person.

Life stage

Peak earning

You're earning more than you ever have, with teenagers or college-age kids at home, and retirement finally starting to feel real.

Worth asking yourself

You're earning the most you ever have — is your savings rate keeping pace, or did it plateau a few years ago?

Start here

  • Redirecting freed-up cash flow, such as post-daycare cash flow, into retirement before lifestyle absorbs it.

What we'll cover

Protection

If your income stopped today, could you still fund the college plans you've already promised your kids?

Peak-earning households often carry the biggest promises made against the least protected income — the gap between commitment and coverage is worth naming directly.

What you can do about it

  • Re-run your life insurance coverage against today's actual college cost estimates, not an old number.
  • Confirm disability coverage would still support tuition payments already committed to.
  • Decide now, not in the moment, what gets cut first if income stopped.
Liquidity

Between paying for college and funding your own retirement, which one wins if you had to choose — and have you actually run that math, or just assumed it works out?

This is usually the first time a family has to prioritize, not just save more. Naming the trade-off out loud is more useful than avoiding it.

What you can do about it

  • Run the numbers on both goals side by side before committing to either.
  • Default to protecting retirement contributions first — retirement has no loans to fall back on.
  • Explore aid, scholarships, or state schools before assuming full price is the only option.
Accumulation

Are you still on pace to hit your retirement number, or has the last several years of family spending quietly pushed that timeline out?

Peak-earning years are also peak-spending years. Checking the pace against the original goal — not just against last year — catches drift early enough to correct it.

What you can do about it

  • Compare your current retirement balance to where your original plan expected it to be at this age.
  • Increase your contribution percentage with each raise instead of letting lifestyle spending absorb it.
  • Revisit your target retirement number — it may have changed since you first set it.
Retirement Income

Do you know, in real numbers, what your household will need per month in retirement — or is it still a guess?

With retirement close enough to plan for but still far enough to adjust, this is the window where a real number replaces a vague feeling.

What you can do about it

  • Estimate your actual monthly expenses in retirement, not just a percentage of current income.
  • Add up expected income sources — Social Security, pensions, withdrawals — against that number.
  • Identify the gap now, while there's still time and income to close it.
Longevity/Legacy

If you became unable to make financial decisions — illness, an accident, cognitive decline — does anyone have the legal authority to step in?

Households at this stage are managing college costs, aging parents, and their own finances at once; a missing power of attorney turns a hard moment into a legal one too.

What you can do about it

  • Set up a durable power of attorney for finances while you're healthy enough to choose who you trust.
  • Add a healthcare directive at the same time — the two decisions usually go together.
  • Tell the person you've named so it isn't a surprise if it's ever needed.

Life stage

Pre-retirement / empty nest

The kids are grown and the house is quieter now — retirement is close enough to plan for, but still far enough that you can adjust course.

Worth asking yourself

You've got a number in your retirement account — but do you know the actual monthly paycheck that number produces?

Start here

  • Translating the account balance into a modeled monthly income projection.

What we'll cover

Protection

If a major health event hit in the next 10 years, would it derail your retirement date?

Protection at this stage isn't about replacing income for decades anymore — it's about keeping one bad year from undoing a decade of planning right before the finish line.

What you can do about it

  • Check whether your health coverage changes at or before your planned retirement date.
  • Build a buffer specifically for a health event, separate from general retirement savings.
  • Revisit disability and supplemental health coverage while you're still working and eligible.
Accumulation

Are you still maximizing catch-up contributions while you have earned income to do it with?

The empty-nest years often free up cash flow right when catch-up limits are available — a short window worth using deliberately, not by accident.

What you can do about it

  • Confirm your plan's catch-up contribution limit and whether you're using it.
  • Increase contributions during these years specifically, since the window is limited.
  • Prioritize catch-up savings over other spending while you still have earned income.
Retirement Income

If you retired on your current savings today, would your income last as long as you might actually live?

"Enough to retire" and "enough to not run out" are two different questions. This stage is the last real window to close that gap while still earning.

What you can do about it

  • Run your savings against a longer time horizon than you expect, not just average life expectancy.
  • Stress-test the plan against a slower withdrawal rate to see how long it actually lasts.
  • Revisit the plan every couple of years as markets and health change.
Retirement Income

Have you mapped out where your income will actually come from month to month in retirement — Social Security, pensions, withdrawals — or is the plan just 'the account will be there'?

A total balance isn't a paycheck. Turning savings into a month-by-month income plan is the work that's easy to defer and expensive to skip.

What you can do about it

  • List every expected income source and the actual month it starts.
  • Map that income against your expected monthly expenses, not your current paycheck.
  • Identify any gap years before Social Security or a pension kicks in.
Longevity/Legacy

Do you have a long-term care plan, or is the plan 'hope it doesn't happen'?

This is the last stage where long-term care coverage is still readily available and affordable — waiting until retirement often means the option has already narrowed or closed.

What you can do about it

  • Get a real quote for long-term care coverage now, while you're still insurable.
  • Decide as a couple how care would be paid for if only one of you needed it.
  • Revisit the plan before rates or eligibility change with age.

Life stage

Retirement / legacy

You're living off what you've built, drawing on savings and investments instead of a paycheck, and thinking about what you'll leave behind.

Worth asking yourself

Is your income guaranteed to last as long as you do, or does it depend on the market cooperating?

Start here

  • A beneficiary and document review.

What we'll cover

Protection

Does your spouse know how to manage the household finances if you're not there to do it?

In many households one partner has handled the finances for decades. Protection here means transferring that knowledge, not just the assets.

What you can do about it

  • Walk your spouse through every account, login, and advisor contact together.
  • Write down the plan in one place, not just in your head.
  • Introduce your spouse to your advisor now, while both of you can ask questions.
Liquidity

Do you have accessible cash outside of investments, so a market downturn doesn't force you to sell at a bad time?

A cash buffer is what turns a market drop from an emergency into a non-event — it buys time to wait the market out instead of selling into it.

What you can do about it

  • Keep a cash reserve outside your investment accounts sized to a year or more of expenses.
  • Replenish that buffer during up markets instead of waiting until it's empty.
  • Treat the buffer as insurance against selling low, not as idle money to invest.
Retirement Income

If markets dropped 20% in your first few years of retirement, do you have a plan for that — or does your whole plan assume steady growth?

Early-retirement downturns do outsized damage because withdrawals compound the loss. A sequence-of-returns plan matters more here than the average return ever will.

What you can do about it

  • Keep 1-2 years of expenses in cash or stable assets so a downturn doesn't force selling investments low.
  • Build flexibility into withdrawals so you can pull back spending in a down year.
  • Plan your withdrawal strategy for down markets specifically, not just up ones.
Longevity/Legacy

Is your estate plan actually up to date, and does your family know where to find it?

Plans drafted decades ago often no longer reflect current wishes, accounts, or family circumstances — and a plan no one can locate works the same as no plan at all.

What you can do about it

  • Pull out your current will, trust, and beneficiary forms and confirm the names on them are still accurate.
  • Tell your family where the documents are kept, not just that they exist.
  • Update anything drafted before a major life change — a death, divorce, or new grandchild.
Longevity/Legacy

If you needed long-term care for several years, whose savings pays for it — yours, or what you meant to leave your kids?

Without a plan, long-term care is funded out of whatever's left — quietly converting a legacy into a care bill.

What you can do about it

  • Decide explicitly whether long-term care would be self-funded, insured, or split with family.
  • Put that decision in writing so family isn't guessing during a crisis.
  • Revisit the plan if health, savings, or family circumstances change.
